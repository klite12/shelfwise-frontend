1929 — Andrew Ross Sorkin

A 592-page narrative history of the Wall Street Crash from the author of Too Big to Fail, built from eight years of research into diaries, correspondence, and records never woven into the story before. It reached #1 on the New York Times nonfiction bestseller list.

Quick summary

1929 tells the story of the Wall Street Crash and the Great Depression that followed it, not as an economics textbook but as a character-driven drama about the bankers, speculators, and regulators who lived through it. Sorkin — previously known for Too Big to Fail, his account of the 2008 financial crisis — spent eight years digging through diaries, personal correspondence, and unpublished papers to reconstruct the boom and bust almost hour by hour in places.

Who it's for

Good fit

  • Readers who enjoyed Too Big to Fail or similar character-driven financial history
  • Anyone curious how closely today's market euphoria echoes 1929's
  • People who prefer narrative nonfiction over dry economic analysis

Not a fit

  • Readers wanting rigorous economic theory over storytelling
  • Anyone put off by a large cast of characters to track across 592 pages
  • Readers sensitive to ornate, occasionally purple prose — several critics flagged this specifically

What critics actually said

Reception has been genuinely mixed rather than uniformly glowing, which is worth knowing before you buy. The Wall Street Journal called it one of the best narrative histories they'd read, and Walter Isaacson praised it as mesmerizing from beginning to end. But The New York Times Book Review was more measured, arguing Sorkin doesn't fully deliver on his most ambitious claims, while still finding real "true-crime" thrills in the storytelling. Book Marks, which aggregates major press reviews, rates the overall critical consensus as positive — but not without real dissent about the book's prose style and its handling of the crash's political implications.

Format and pricing

1929 is available in hardcover, Kindle, and audiobook formats. As a major nonfiction release from a big-five publisher (Viking), pricing follows typical hardcover/Kindle patterns rather than a fixed number — Kindle editions of books like this are frequently discounted from the hardcover list price, so it's worth checking the current listing rather than assuming a fixed figure.

Pros and cons

Pros
  • Genuinely deep research — eight years, with previously unpublished material
  • Character-driven approach makes 1929 feel immediate rather than distant history
  • Strong blurbs from Pulitzer-winning historians, not just marketing copy
  • Parallels to current market conditions give it real present-day relevance
Cons
  • Some critics found the prose overwrought or cliché-heavy in places
  • At 592 pages with a large cast, it's a genuine time commitment, not a quick read
  • The New York Times Book Review felt it fell short of its most ambitious analytical goals
Final verdict

A well-researched, imperfect, genuinely gripping read

1929 earns its bestseller status on the strength of its storytelling and research, not because critics were unanimous. If you want narrative history that reads like a drama and don't mind some stylistic excess along the way, it delivers. If you're after tight economic analysis, you may find yourself agreeing with its more skeptical reviewers.
